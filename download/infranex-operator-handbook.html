The Golden Path — Workflow at a Glance

Infranex BT is a Bittensor mining operations platform: it watches the TAO chain, rents GPU capacity from providers, drives your miners through a node daemon, and continuously tests each miner against a simulated validator to tell you exactly what to fix. The whole product is built around one repeating loop, and every view in the sidebar exists to serve one step of that loop. This chapter gives you the map; the rest of the handbook walks each step in full detail, in the exact order you should perform them.

The single most important ordering rule in this handbook is this: the daemon must be online before you run the Validator Lab’s Apply step. Mining a validator profile runs on the platform server, so it works even with no GPU attached — but the moment you click Apply, the platform pushes a config change to a real machine through that machine’s daemon. No daemon, no push. Follow the phases in order and this can never bite you.

Pick your subnet
Subnets · Opportunities
Sync live chain data, compare ROI, deregistration risk and free seats, and choose the netuid you want to mine.
Output: a chosen subnet (netuid)

3

The one-liner to remember: Subnet → GPU → daemon online → Validate → Apply → then it is hands-off until an alert tells you to re-validate. If you ever wonder “when do I run the Validator Lab?” — the answer is after the daemon chip turns green, and again whenever Monitoring or the DevOps Engine says something drifted.

Phase 0 · One-time setup
Before You Touch Any Subnet

Three things must be in place before the mining loop can run: an account on the platform, a provider API key so the platform can rent GPUs on your behalf, and a Bittensor wallet for on-chain registration. Each is a five-minute job, and you only do them once. If you inherited a working install, skim this chapter anyway — it tells you which keys the later phases depend on, so you know what breaks if one is missing.

1
Log in to the platform
Open the web app and enter your User ID and Access Code. The code is stored hashed and encrypted; there is no recovery flow, so keep the original from your operator. Successful login lands you on the Dashboard with live chain data (block height, TAO price, subnet count) already streaming.
2
Connect a provider key
Go to GPU Catalog and click Connect provider key. Paste a RunPod API key (and a Vast.ai key if you plan to compare both). Until a key is configured the offers list is empty — the header will say no live offers yet as a hint. With a key present you get live pricing: roughly 45 GPU types with real-time hourly rates.
3
Have your wallet ready
The Deploy Wizard’s last step registers a hotkey on-chain, which needs your coldkey mnemonic / seed (or an existing hotkey pair). The platform walks you through registration in Phase 2 step 5 — you only need the seed phrase available, funded with a small TAO balance for the registration fee if the subnet charges one.
Checkpoint — you are ready when

The Dashboard renders live chain numbers, GPU Catalog shows live offers from your provider, and your wallet seed is at hand. Nothing here involves a GPU machine yet.

Phase 1 · Research
Pick Your Subnet

A subnet is a market: it defines what kind of miner you must run, how seats are priced, and how brutal the competition is. Choosing well matters more than any later tuning — a good miner on a bad subnet loses money on electricity it never earns back. The Subnets view (with Opportunities as its aggressive cousin) gives you the live ranking; your job here is to shortlist one netuid and note its requirements.

1
Sync the chain data
Open Subnets and click Sync all. The table refreshes from the chain RPC every ~30 seconds on its own, but an explicit sync guarantees the seat counts and emission numbers you are about to evaluate are current, not cached.
2
Filter to active subnets
Use the active-only filter chips to hide dead or speculative subnets. There is no point evaluating a subnet whose emission is zero or whose registration is effectively closed — the filter does that pre-work for you.
3
Compare the three decision numbers
For each candidate read: ROI score (estimated earnings against current cost), dereg risk (how likely underperformers are purged — high risk means high churn and high skill floor), and free seats (whether registration can even succeed). The Opportunities view adds cross-subnet arbitrage if several subnets look close.
4
Choose and write it down
Pick one netuid and note it together with the subnet’s minimum VRAM from the requirements shown later — you will type the netuid into the Validator Lab and the Deploy Wizard, so having it in front of you avoids mistakes.
Checkpoint — you are done when

You have one netuid chosen and a rough expectation of its VRAM floor. Do not over-research: the Validator Lab in Phase 4 will verify the economics against your actual machine anyway.

Phase 2 · Deploy
Deploy the GPU — the Five-Step Wizard

Everything in this phase happens inside one dialog: the Deploy Wizard. It replaces what used to be scattered across three screens — nothing is out of order or missing, because rent, install, and registration flow end-to-end. You can enter it two ways: from GPU Catalog, press Provision on the offer you like (the GPU arrives preselected), or from Subnets / Opportunities, press start-mining on the card (the subnet arrives preselected and the wizard opens directly on the requirements step). Either way, the five steps below run in the same order.

1
Step 1 — Choose the subnet
A live chain list with seat verdicts per subnet. Pick the netuid you chose in Phase 1, or let a preselection stand. The wizard shows whether registration is realistic before it lets you spend money.
2
Step 2 — Check the requirements
The wizard fetches the subnet’s real requirements profile and displays the minimum VRAM and model expectations. Do not skip this check — renting a GPU below the VRAM floor is the single most common wasted-rent mistake, and the recommendation card on GPU Catalog repeats the same warning for H100s.
3
Step 3 — Pick the GPU offer
Live offers from your configured providers, filterable by provider and max price, sortable by hourly price or VRAM. Match or exceed the VRAM floor from step 2, sanity-check the hourly rate against the subnet’s expected emission, and select the offer.
4
Step 4 — Rent and auto-install
Confirming the rent creates the deployment and starts a real ten-step install runner with a live log. You can literally watch each step complete; the lifecycle auto-advances on the platform server even if you close the tab, and when the deployment status flips to started the wizard moves itself to the final step.
5
Step 5 — Hotkey & run
The wallet registration hand-off. Follow the registration wizard (coldkey seed → hotkey creation → on-chain register) to put a live key on the subnet. When it completes, close the dialog — a deployment card now exists in Deployments with monthly cost, estimated revenue, and lifecycle progress.
Checkpoint — you are done when

Deployments lists your new card in state started with cost/revenue figures populated. The machine exists and the miner stack is installed — but the platform still cannot reach inside it. That is the daemon’s job, next.

Note on mock deployments: if you were experimenting without a real provider key, the platform can create a simulated deployment that ticks through the same lifecycle. Mocks are clearly labeled, but remember they cannot serve real traffic and should not be mixed into a production fleet’s dashboards.

Phase 3 · Connect
Connect the Node Daemon

The daemon is a tiny agent that runs on your GPU pod and polls the platform for work. Everything downstream — Validator Lab’s Apply, DevOps automated responses, config edits from the deployment card — travels through it. Installing it is the one genuinely manual moment in the whole workflow, and even that is a single copy-paste. The dialog itself summarizes the contract: SSH as root → paste one command → wait about 60 seconds → the chip turns Online.

1
Open the install dialog
In Deployments, find your deployment card and click Install daemon. The dialog shows the pre-built setup command with the daemon identity and secret already embedded for this specific deployment — never reuse one deployment’s command on another machine.
2
Copy the setup command
Click Copy setup command (auto-start service). The command is self-contained and idempotent: it writes the daemon, installs an auto-start service, and cleans up any older install. On a VM or bare metal it registers a systemd unit (survives reboots, restarts on crash); inside containers like RunPod or Vast it falls back to a watchdog loop that relaunches the daemon every 15 seconds.
3
Run it on the GPU pod
SSH into the pod as root and paste the command into the shell. The pod prints which launcher it chose — launcher=systemd or launcher=watchdog — so you always know what is keeping it alive.
4
Watch for Online
Within about a minute the deployment card’s daemon chip flips to Online (the platform shows a “last seen” age in seconds once heartbeats arrive). If it stays offline after several minutes, jump to the troubleshooting chapter — the usual causes are a wrong platform URL inside the pod or an egress firewall.
5
Optional — automate it away
To never SSH again: paste the same setup command into your provider’s start script instead (RunPod: template → Start script; Vast.ai: on-start script; any cloud: cloud-init user-data). Every fresh boot then installs or refreshes the daemon automatically. Use one template per deployment — the embedded identity and secret are deployment-bound, so sharing a template across machines would give them one identity.
Checkpoint — you are done when

The daemon chip on the deployment card is green Online with a fresh “last seen”. From this moment, Validator Lab Apply has somewhere real to push to.

Updating later: re-paste the setup command any time — it is idempotent, so re-running it upgrades the daemon in place (systemd restarts the service; the watchdog replaces the old process). The dialog also carries an uninstall command if you ever decommission the pod.

Phase 4 · Verify
Validator Lab — Test, Read the Verdict, Apply

The Validator Lab is the platform’s dress rehearsal: it mines a profile of what your target subnet’s validator actually rewards, simulates your miner against it, and returns a verdict with per-dimension scores — quality, resource fit, throughput, speed, price, availability. Each weak dimension comes with a concrete recommendation, and each recommendation carries an Apply button that pushes the fix to your live machine. This is the loop you will re-enter many times over the miner’s life, so learn it once here.

1
Select the subnet
Open Validator Lab and pick the same netuid your deployment mines. The Lab’s recommendations are subnet-specific — a fix computed for one subnet’s validator may be wrong for another.
2
Mine the profile (first time only)
If no profile exists yet the panel says so — press Re-mine profile and wait while the platform extracts the subnet’s reward rules. This runs server-side and does not need your GPU. Re-mine again later whenever the subnet’s upstream code changes.
3
Run the simulation
Press Run simulation. While it scores, the button reads Scoring against validator…. When it finishes you get a Verdict panel: overall verdict badge, composite score, per-dimension scores, and a ranked list of the highest-leverage fixes.
4
Apply the fixes you accept
Click Apply on a recommendation. A picker lists your deployments — same-subnet ones sorted first — with a preview of the computed change (for price fixes, the exact target USD per M-token). Confirm, and the platform maps the fix to a recipe (for example: quality → fp8 weights plus self-verify; throughput → vLLM; price → the computed ask price), snapshots the current revision, then pushes apply_config to the daemon. A result note confirms the push; mock deployments receive a simulated tick instead.
5
Know that verdicts survive
The Recent runs list keeps every simulation. Click any row to restore that verdict and its Apply buttons — nothing is lost by navigating away or by the page reloading. Availability fixes are deliberately manual; the Lab will say so instead of pretending to automate them.
Checkpoint — you are done when

At least one fix is applied, the deployment card’s config tab shows the changed environment, and a new revision snapshot exists for rollback. The miner now runs the validator-approved configuration.

When should you run the Validator Lab? The table below is the complete answer — first time is immediately after the daemon comes online, and every later run is triggered by one of four events. If none of these happened, there is nothing to re-validate; spend the time on Monitoring instead.

MomentWhy
Right after the daemon turns OnlineFirst configuration pass. Apply needs a live deployment, so this is the earliest useful moment — profile mining alone can run earlier, but it produces nothing actionable without a connected miner.
A DevOps Engine alert firesGPU HEALTH, SUBNET DRIFT, BENCH REGRESS and friends tell you reality moved; re-run the simulation and apply fresh fixes.

Phase 5 · Operate
Operate — Monitor, Automate, Re-Validate

Once a fix is applied, the system is designed to run without you. Monitoring is the health picture: inference scores, service latency, GPU health, query volume, and earnings against cost, all updating continuously. The DevOps Engine is the action layer on top: fourteen trigger types watch the same telemetry and respond automatically — restarting a sick miner, re-syncing after an upstream change, scaling capacity, or escalating to you when a decision is genuinely yours. Your daily job shrinks to reading one screen and letting the engine do the rest.

Monitoring — what to glance at daily
  • Composite / inference score — the miner’s actual standing; a slow slide means the validator profile aged.
  • Daemon heartbeats — “last seen” age should stay in seconds, never minutes.
  • Cost vs estimated revenue — the deployment card’s two headline numbers must stay in healthy proportion.
  • Alert stream — anything the DevOps Engine escalated to a human.
DevOps Engine — the 14 triggers

GPU HEALTH · SUBNET DRIFT · DEREG-RISK · ARBITRAGE · RUNTIME OPT · PROBE FAIL · SERVICE LATENCY · QUERY DROUGHT · ESCALATION · UPSTREAM · BENCH REGRESS · RE-SYNC · SCALE · KILL. Each trigger documents what it watches and what it does; treat UPSTREAM and BENCH REGRESS as your “go re-validate” signals, and KILL as the engine’s last-resort circuit breaker for a runaway deployment.

Reading alerts correctly: an alert names a symptom, not a culprit. A data-source hiccup (chain RPC, price feed) can look identical to a broken miner in a dashboard — before touching the machine, check the System & Errors view to confirm the platform’s own feeds are healthy. This one habit prevents the most common false-alarm fire drills.

Recovery
If Something Goes Wrong

Failures have a natural diagnosis order: rule out the platform itself first, then the pipeline, then the machine, then the config. Work top to bottom — each step either finds the problem or definitively excludes a layer, which is exactly what you want when an alert is screaming and TAO is burning. The single most valuable habit is resisting the urge to redeploy from scratch; almost nothing in this stack needs that.

1
Check the platform’s own health
Open System & Errors. If chain RPC, price feeds, or provider APIs show failures there, the “miner problem” in Monitoring may be a data-source outage wearing a costume. Wait it out or re-sync before deeper digging.
2
Read Monitoring for scope
One miner sick or the whole fleet? One subnet or all? Scope tells you where to look next: a single deployment points at the machine or its config; everything-at-once points at the platform or a global data source.
3
Inspect the deployment card
Open the card’s logs and config tabs. The logs show the install runner and daemon activity; the config tab shows the exact environment the miner is running — compare it against what the last Validator Lab apply claimed to set.
4
Roll back with Revisions
If the card got sick right after an apply, open the Revisions dialog and restore the previous snapshot. Every apply creates one precisely so this step is one click, never archaeology.
5
Re-install the daemon if it vanished
Daemon chip gray? Re-paste the same setup command from Install daemon — it is idempotent and will cleanly reinstall or upgrade the agent. On spot/ephemeral machines the pod may have been evicted entirely; redeploy from Phase 2.

Cadence
Operating Rhythms & Quick Reference

The platform rewards a calm operator more than a busy one. The rhythms below are the entire ongoing workload once your first miner is tuned: a one-time setup measured in minutes, a daily glance of roughly five, and a weekly fifteen-minute review. Everything else is event-driven — the DevOps Engine pings you when reality changes and stays silent when it hasn’t.

First-time setup~30 minutes, oncePhase 0 keys → pick subnet → run the Deploy Wizard → daemon online → validate → apply the top fixes.
Daily~5 minutesGlance at Monitoring: composite, daemon ages, cost vs revenue, escalations. Touch nothing that is green.
Weekly~15 minutesRe-run Validator Lab, compare the composite with last week, re-mine the profile if upstream changed, apply drifts. Skim System & Errors for recurring warnings.
On alertas neededFollow the recovery order: System & Errors → Monitoring scope → card logs/config → Revisions rollback → daemon re-paste.

Strengths and cautions, honestly stated: the platform’s strongest assets are the honest transmission labeling (mock vs live is never blurred), the revision snapshots behind every apply, and the closed Validate→Apply loop. Its sharp edges: preview URLs can leak into copied configs, mock deployments can pollute fleet views if you experiment in the same workspace, spot instances can be evicted mid-run (prefer persistent pods for long-lived miners), and a data-source outage can masquerade as a miner failure — always check System & Errors before surgery.

The Whole Handbook in One Line
Mine. Validate. Apply.
Repeat.
The golden path never changes: pick a subnet worth mining, put a real GPU under it, connect the daemon so the platform has hands on the machine, let the Validator Lab find the gap between your miner and the validator’s expectations, and apply the fixes with one click. After that, Monitoring and the DevOps Engine keep watch — and you only return when an alert, an upstream change, or the weekly review calls you back.
